## B. Look under the hood at the estimated network

> *"Ville det give mening at kigge på koefficientestimaterne, altså gamma_{i,j}?"* and
> *"A_t kan ses som faktorer der læres (ikke-lineært) af (T_t,P_t)... plotte dem?
> Og estimaterne for beta_1 og beta_2."*

**This is already computed.** Extracted from
`results/paper/weights/global_model/parameters/(2,).weights.h5` (activation is **swish**, not
sigmoid; inputs are standardised with mean T = 18.05 °C, sd = 7.10; mean P = 1094.3 mm, sd = 678.3).

First layer, `NN_k = c_k + gamma_{k,T} * T~ + gamma_{k,P} * P~`:

| neuron | c_k | gamma_T | gamma_P | \|gT\|/\|gP\| | direction |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | −0.1223 | −1.5749 | 0.1743 | 9.0 | 173.7° |
| 2 | 0.2148 | −0.3996 | 0.0222 | 18.0 | 176.8° |

Output layer, `y = beta_1*A_1 + beta_2*A_2 + FE + trends`:
**beta_1 = −0.1517, beta_2 = +0.5253** (no output bias — the intercept is absorbed by the within
transformation).

In natural units the pre-activation indices are
`NN_1 = 3.601 − 0.2218*T[°C] + 0.2569*P[m]` and `NN_2 = 1.195 − 0.0563*T[°C] + 0.0328*P[m]`.

### What this says (three findings worth putting in the paper)

1. **Both neurons are temperature-dominated and nearly collinear.** In standardised units
   temperature outweighs precipitation 9:1 and 18:1, and the two index directions differ by only
   **3.1°** (cosine similarity 0.9985). The BIC-selected global model is therefore, to a very good
   approximation, a **single-index (ridge) model**: `f(T,P) ≈ h(gamma'x)`. The two neurons are not
   two distinct climate mechanisms; they build a flexible *univariate* shape along one climate
   index. The genuine T×P interaction in the surface comes from the modest difference in the two
   precipitation loadings.
2. **An interpretable trade-off.** Along neuron 1's index, one additional metre of annual
   precipitation offsets roughly **1.16 °C** of warming (0.2569/0.2218); along neuron 2, roughly
   0.58 °C. This is a quotable, economically meaningful number and it is the micro-foundation of
   the paper's existing claim that precipitation moderates the temperature response.
3. **The curvature is generated by near-cancellation.** The signed temperature contributions
   `beta_k * gamma_{k,T}` are **+0.239** and **−0.210** — nearly equal and opposite. The fitted
   response is a small difference between two larger, shifted swish ridges along almost the same
   index. This is the standard mechanism by which a two-neuron network produces a hump, and it
   explains why BIC stops at two neurons.

**Important caveat to state explicitly.** Individual `gamma` and `beta` are only weakly
identified: the model is invariant to permuting the two neurons, and the near-cancellation in (3)
means many `(gamma, beta)` combinations produce nearly the same surface. So the paper should lead
with *surface-level* objects (the index direction, the implied precipitation-temperature
trade-off) and report the raw coefficients with that caveat — not the other way round. This also
plausibly explains part of the width of the bootstrap bands (WS2): the surface can be far better
identified than the parameters that generate it.

**Steps**
1. Re-fit `(2,)` with many random initialisations (say 50) and record `(gamma, beta)` and the
   fitted surface for each. Show that the surface is stable while the parameters are not — that
   turns the caveat into evidence and is a genuinely interesting methodological point.
2. Compute the learned factors `A_{k,it} = swish(NN_k)` for every country-year, then plot:
   (a) the cross-country mean of `A_1` and `A_2` by year against mean `T` and `P` by year;
   (b) `A_1`, `A_2` for a handful of representative countries (e.g. a cold-rich, a hot-poor and a
   wet-tropical one) to show the cross-sectional spread.
   Note for the supervisor: `A` is indexed by country *and* year, so a pure time-series plot
   requires an aggregation choice — the cross-country mean is the natural analogue of what he
   sketched.
3. Report `beta_1`, `beta_2` as factor loadings alongside the factor plots.

**Deliverable.** A short "what the network learns" subsection (this is the *interpretability*
answer reviewers of ML-in-economics papers always want) plus a two-panel figure and a small
coefficient table with the identification caveat.

### Results (computed 2026-08-25)

`scripts/2026-08-25-learned-factors.py` → `paper/Figures/Interpretability/learned_factors.pdf`
and `results/tables/learned_factors_panel.csv` (factors for all 10,324 country-years).

**The two factors are nearly the same object.** `corr(A_1, A_2) = 0.961` across country-years
(0.967 in balanced annual means). Both are essentially *coldness* indices: `corr(A_k, T)` =
−0.913 and −0.989, versus only −0.29 and −0.31 with precipitation. This is the empirical
counterpart of the 3.1° angle between the two index directions and confirms the single-index
reading.

**The near-cancellation is severe.** `sd(beta_1 A_1) = 0.1386` and `sd(beta_2 A_2) = 0.1337`, but
the net climate contribution has `sd = 0.0381` — each component varies about **3.6x more than
their sum**. The fitted climate effect is a small residual of two much larger, nearly-offsetting
terms. This is a quantitative statement of the mechanism and a strong argument for reporting
surface-level objects rather than raw coefficients. It is also a plausible contributor to the
width of the bootstrap bands (WS2).

**Composition warning (important, and it bit us).** The panel grows from 103 countries (1961) to
194 (2010), jumping by 18 between 1990 and 1991 (Soviet breakup). Averaged over the *unbalanced*
panel, mean temperature appears to **fall** by about 1 °C around 1991 and the factors jump — pure
composition. All aggregate plots therefore use a **balanced sub-panel of 96 countries** with full
63-year coverage, on which mean temperature **rises +1.13 °C** (18.36 → 19.50) over 1961-2023 and
both factors decline monotonically (A_1 0.287 → 0.195, A_2 0.140 → 0.104), exactly as the negative
`gamma_T` implies. Any future country-year aggregation in this project must apply the same
correction.

**Still outstanding:** the multi-initialisation stability check requires TensorFlow, which the
sandbox lacks — it has to run locally or on the cluster (task #42).

## C. Why not just use existing nonparametric methods? (Orimar's point)

> *"Hvorfor bruger I ikke bare de ikke-parametriske modeller, som er tilgængelige i litteraturen?
> Jeg synes vi skal være skarp på dette."*

Finding B gives the sharpest possible answer, because it reframes the question: **the selected
model essentially is a classical nonparametric estimator.** A two-neuron single-hidden-layer
network is a two-term **projection-pursuit regression** (Friedman and Stuetzle, 1981), and finding
B shows it collapses further to approximately a single-index model. So we are not avoiding the
nonparametric literature — we are estimating an object squarely inside it, with three properties
off-the-shelf implementations lack:

1. **The panel nuisance structure.** We concentrate out roughly `3N + T` fixed effects and trends
   with a rank-deficient design, *inside* the estimation, as an untrained within layer. Standard
   GAM, kernel and spline packages have no mechanism for this; the whole point of the paper is
   that the climate response is compared to Burke on an identical fixed-effect footing.
2. **Scaling in the input dimension.** Kernel and full tensor-product spline methods degrade
   quickly as inputs are added. This is not hypothetical: it is exactly what blocks the dynamic
   extension in D, where `t` becomes a third input, and any further covariate a fourth.
3. **Interactions without choosing a basis.** A GAM is additive by construction; capturing T×P
   requires explicitly specifying a tensor product or interaction term, which re-introduces the
   functional-form choice the paper exists to avoid.

**Do not leave this rhetorical.** The strongest response is empirical: estimate **one** off-the-shelf
nonparametric alternative — a tensor-product spline or a GAM with an explicit T×P interaction —
within the same within-transformed framework, and show it produces a similar surface. If it
agrees, the network is validated and the choice becomes one of convenience and extensibility; if
it disagrees, that is itself a finding. Either way the referee question is closed with evidence.

**Deliverable.** A tightened paragraph in the methodology (positioning against projection pursuit,
GAMs, kernel regression, with citations) plus a robustness appendix containing the spline
comparison.

## D. The dynamic model (time as a network input)

> *"Vi har stadig ikke forsøgt med den dynamisk model... Timo foreslog at lade tidsvektoren indgå
> som input... Den ville tillade at fladen ændrer sig henover tid. Noget som hverken Burke eller
> Yuan/Leirvik har overvejet."*

**It has in fact already been run.** `runs/estimation/2026-07-02_12-01-08_global_dynamic`:
`dynamic_model: true`, `within_projection: true`, `formulation: global`, swish, country trends,
BIC selection, **all 55 architectures completed**.

Results (first value BIC, second AIC):

| architecture | BIC | AIC |
|---|---|---|
| (2, 2) | **−53074.3** | −57448.6 |
| (2,) | −53049.9 | −57380.8 |
| (2, 2, 2) | −53008.0 | −57425.8 |
| (4,) | −53005.3 | −57408.6 |

The BIC-best dynamic specification is `(2,2)` at **−53074.3**. The static model reported in the
paper is `(2,)` at **−53270.4**. Taken at face value the **static model is preferred by roughly
196 BIC points** — decisive against time variation.

**Before reporting that, one thing must be checked.** The dynamic implementation *drops the time
fixed effects* (`include_time = not dynamic_model` in `multivariate_model.py:179`), letting the
network absorb common time variation through `t` instead. So the comparison currently conflates
two different questions: "does the climate surface vary over time?" and "are time fixed effects
needed?". Since the dynamic model has ~63 *fewer* nuisance parameters and BIC still prefers the
static one, the dynamic fit must be substantially worse — consistent with a smooth function of `t`
being a poor substitute for 64 unrestricted year effects (oil shocks, the financial crisis, COVID).

**Identification point for the write-up.** Even with year effects dropped, a common linear or
quadratic component of `f(T,P,t)` lies in the span of the country trends, so it is absorbed. The
dynamic surface is therefore identified only through **interaction between `t` and `(T,P)`** — that
is, through changes in the *shape* of the response, not its level. Say this explicitly; it is
subtle and a referee will look for it.

**GATING CHECK RESOLVED (2026-08-25): the comparison is legitimate.**
`multivariate_model.py:290` computes `m_eff = self.m + proj.rank` in within mode, so the parameter
count already includes `rank(Delta)` — which correctly differs between the runs because the
dynamic path drops the time dummies. Both runs use the same sample (`data_source: wb`,
`data_end: 2024`, n = 10,324) and the same full-fit MSE. Confirmed values:

| | static `2026-06-13_22-11-06` | dynamic `2026-07-02_12-01-08` |
|---|---|---|
| best BIC | `(2,)` **−53270.37** | `(2,2)` **−53074.31** |
| best AIC | `(2,2)` **−58032.92** | `(2,2)` −57448.62 |

Static is preferred by **196 BIC points and 584 AIC points** — both criteria, decisively. The
negative result is reportable.

**Steps**
1. ~~Verify `k_m` comparability~~ — done, see above.
2. If comparable, the honest headline is a **negative result**: allowing the surface to move over
   time is not supported by BIC. That is publishable and directly answers the adaptation question.
3. Consider the properly identified variant: **keep the year fixed effects** and let `t` enter the
   network only through interactions with `(T,P)` (or de-mean the network output by year). This
   isolates "does the shape change" from "are year effects needed", and is the specification that
   actually tests adaptation.
4. Visualise regardless: the surface at three dates (e.g. 1975, 1995, 2020) and the
   growth-maximising temperature as a function of `t`.

**Framing.** This is a test for **adaptation** — whether the damage function has shifted over six
decades — which connects directly to Burke and Emerick (2016), already in the bibliography, and
which neither Burke nor Karabiyik/Leirvik/Yuan attempt. If the surface is stable, "no detectable
adaptation over 1960-2024" is a clean contribution. If it shifts, it is a headline result and
plausibly its own paper.

**Scope.** Supervisor flags it may not belong in this paper. Recommendation: complete steps 1-2
(cheap — the run exists), report the BIC comparison as a short subsection or appendix, and defer
the properly identified variant (step 3) to a decision after seeing it.
